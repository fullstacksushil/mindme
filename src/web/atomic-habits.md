# Atomic Habits
> A 2018 book by James Clear about how tiny changes in behavior add up to remarkable results. It explains how habits form and gives four laws for building good habits and breaking bad ones.
- Why small habits matter
  > Habits are the compound interest of self-improvement. A small change looks like nothing on the day you make it, and makes a large difference over years.
  - Getting 1% better
    > Improving by 1% a day is barely noticeable, but it compounds the way money does.
    - The math of compounding
      > The same tiny change, repeated every day for a year, moves you a long way in either direction.
      - 1% better every day
        > 1.01 to the power of 365 is about 37. A year of small gains leaves you nearly 37 times better.
        - Why it feels slow
          > Most of the gain arrives late, so the early days look like no progress at all.
      - 1% worse every day
        > 0.99 to the power of 365 is about 0.03. Small daily slips bring you close to zero.
        - Bad habits compound too
          > A little junk food or a little procrastination costs almost nothing today, and a lot over years.
  - The Plateau of Latent Potential
    > Results lag behind effort. The work you put in is stored up before it shows.
    - The Valley of Disappointment
      > The gap between where you expect to be and where you are. Many people quit here.
      - The ice cube
        > An ice cube in a cold room does not melt from 25 to 31 degrees Fahrenheit. At 32 it starts to melt. The earlier degrees were not wasted.
        - Breakthroughs
          > A breakthrough is often the result of many earlier actions that built up the potential for it.
  - Systems over goals
    > Goals are the results you want. Systems are the processes that lead to them.
    - Problems with goals
      > Clear argues that goals alone do not explain success, and that they can work against you.
      - Winners and losers share goals
        > Every competitor wants to win. The goal cannot be what sets the winner apart.
      - A goal is a moment
        > Reaching a goal changes your life only for a moment. The habits behind it are what last.
        - The yo-yo effect
          > Once a goal is reached, the motivation behind it is gone, and people slide back.
      - Goals delay happiness
        > "I'll be happy once I reach my goal" puts happiness off until a single future day.
    - You fall to the level of your systems
      > You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems.
- Identity-based habits
  > The most lasting change starts with who you believe you are, not with what you want to achieve.
  - Three layers of behavior change
    > Change can happen at three levels, from the surface inward.
    - Outcomes
      > Changing your results, such as losing weight or writing a book. This is what you get.
    - Processes
      > Changing your habits and systems, such as a new gym routine. This is what you do.
    - Identity
      > Changing your beliefs about yourself. This is what you believe, and it is the deepest layer.
      - Outcome-based habits
        > Starting from what you want to achieve. The habit lasts only as long as the goal does.
      - Identity-based habits
        > Starting from who you want to become. "I'm not a smoker" holds better than "I'm trying to quit."
  - Every action is a vote
    > Each time you act a certain way, you cast a vote for the type of person you want to become.
    - Evidence builds belief
      > The more often you repeat a behavior, the more evidence you have that it is who you are.
      - Small wins count
        > You do not need a unanimous vote. You need a majority, built from many small actions.
  - The two-step process
    > Habits shape identity, and identity shapes habits. Clear suggests using that loop on purpose.
    - Decide who you want to be
      > Ask what kind of person could get the result you want.
    - Prove it with small wins
      > Act like that person in small ways, every day, until the belief follows.
- The habit loop
  > Every habit follows the same four steps, repeated until they run on their own.
  - Cue
    > The trigger that starts the behavior. It predicts a reward.
  - Craving
    > The motivation behind the habit. You do not crave the habit, you crave the change in state it brings.
  - Response
    > The habit you actually perform, as a thought or an action.
  - Reward
    > The end goal of every habit. It satisfies the craving and teaches your brain which actions are worth remembering.
  - Two phases
    > The loop splits into a problem phase and a solution phase.
    - Problem phase
      > Cue and craving. You notice that something needs to change.
    - Solution phase
      > Response and reward. You act and get what you wanted.
- The Four Laws of Behavior Change
  > Each law matches one step of the habit loop. To break a bad habit, invert the law.
  - 1st Law: Make it obvious
    > Matches the cue. The easier a cue is to notice, the more likely the habit.
    - The Habits Scorecard
      > Write down your daily habits and mark each one as good, bad or neutral, to become aware of them.
      - Pointing-and-calling
        > Japanese train operators point at signals and call out their status. Saying an action out loud makes you aware of it.
    - Implementation intentions
      > A plan for when and where you will act: "I will [behavior] at [time] in [location]."
      - Why it works
        > A specific plan turns a vague wish into a decision you have already made.
    - Habit stacking
      > Pair a new habit with one you already do: "After [current habit], I will [new habit]."
      - Choosing the right cue
        > The current habit should happen at the right time and place, and as often as the new one.
        - Too vague
          > "After I read a book" happens too irregularly to anchor a daily habit.
        - Specific and frequent
          > "After I pour my morning coffee" happens every day, at the same moment.
    - Environment design
      > Make the cues of good habits visible, so your surroundings prompt the behavior.
      - The water bottle study
        > A hospital cafeteria sold more water and less soda after water was placed in more visible spots, with no other change.
      - One space, one use
        > Tie a place to one habit, such as a chair only for reading, so the space itself becomes the cue.
    - Inversion: make it invisible
      > Remove the cues of bad habits from your environment. Self-control is a short-term strategy.
  - 2nd Law: Make it attractive
    > Matches the craving. The more attractive a habit, the more likely you are to do it.
    - Dopamine and anticipation
      > Dopamine rises when you expect a reward, not only when you get it. Wanting drives action.
    - Temptation bundling
      > Pair something you need to do with something you want to do.
      - Premack's principle
        > More probable behaviors reinforce less probable ones. Do the habit you need, then the one you want.
    - The culture you live in
      > We pick up habits from the people around us.
      - The close
        > We imitate family and friends.
      - The many
        > We follow what most people around us do.
      - The powerful
        > We copy people with status and success.
    - Reframe your mindset
      > Say "I get to" instead of "I have to", so the same habit feels like an opportunity.
    - Inversion: make it unattractive
      > Highlight the benefits of avoiding a bad habit, so it looks less appealing.
  - 3rd Law: Make it easy
    > Matches the response. The easier a habit is, the more likely you are to do it.
    - Motion versus action
      > Planning and researching feel productive but produce nothing. Only action delivers a result.
    - Repetition, not time
      > A habit forms through how often you do it, not how long you have been doing it.
      - Automaticity
        > With enough repetitions a behavior becomes automatic, and needs little thought.
    - The Law of Least Effort
      > People drift toward the option that takes the least work.
      - Reduce friction
        > Cut the steps between you and a good habit.
        - Prime the environment
          > Set things up in advance, such as laying out workout clothes the night before.
    - The Two-Minute Rule
      > Scale a new habit down until it takes two minutes or less, such as "read one page."
      - Master showing up
        > A habit must be established before it can be improved. Showing up comes first.
        - Gateway habits
          > The first two minutes lead into the rest of the routine.
    - Commitment devices
      > A choice made now that locks in better behavior later, such as buying food in single portions.
    - Automate it
      > Use technology to make good habits happen without effort, such as automatic savings.
    - Inversion: make it difficult
      > Add friction to bad habits, so they take more steps.
  - 4th Law: Make it satisfying
    > Matches the reward. What is immediately rewarded is repeated. What is immediately punished is avoided.
    - Immediate versus delayed rewards
      > The brain evolved for immediate returns, but most good habits pay off later.
      - Make the reward immediate
        > Add a small, instant reward when you do a good habit.
    - Habit tracking
      > Record each time you do the habit. Watching the streak grow is satisfying in itself.
      - The paper clip strategy
        > Move one paper clip from one jar to another for each repetition, so progress is visible.
      - Never miss twice
        > Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new habit.
    - Inversion: make it unsatisfying
      > Make the cost of a bad habit immediate and visible.
      - Habit contracts
        > A written agreement that names a penalty if you break the habit, signed by you and others.
      - Accountability partners
        > Someone watching makes a lapse cost something right away.
- Advanced tactics
  > How to go from merely good to truly great.
  - Genes and personality
    > Genes do not decide your success, but they decide where your chances are best.
    - Pick the right field
      > Choose habits that suit your natural abilities and interests.
      - Explore, then exploit
        > Try many things at first, then focus on what works best.
  - The Goldilocks Rule
    > People stay most motivated working on tasks at the edge of their current abilities, neither too hard nor too easy.
    - Boredom is the enemy
      > Many habits fail not because they are hard, but because they stop being interesting.
  - The downside of habits
    > Automatic habits can stop you improving, because you stop paying attention.
    - Habits plus deliberate practice
      > Mastery comes from habits combined with deliberate practice on the next small skill.
    - Reflection and review
      > Regular review shows where you are improving and where you have drifted.
      - Annual Review
        > A yearly look at what went well, what did not, and what was learned.
      - Integrity Report
        > A yearly check of whether you are living by your core values.
